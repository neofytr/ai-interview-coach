# Engineering Manager — Interview Guide

## Team Building and People Development

Engineering manager interviews place heavy weight on how candidates build, grow, and retain high-performing teams. Interviewers ask "Tell me about a time you inherited an underperforming team" or "How do you approach hiring?" They evaluate whether the candidate has a deliberate philosophy for team composition — balancing seniority, complementary skills, and diversity of perspective — or whether they hire reactively.

Key competencies: conducting effective 1:1s that surface problems early, giving direct and actionable feedback, creating career development plans that align individual growth with team needs, and building psychological safety so engineers raise concerns before they become crises. Strong candidates describe repeatable approaches for calibration, performance reviews, and promotion cases.

Common pitfall: positioning themselves as the hero who rescued a team. Interviewers look for candidates who credit their team, describe systemic changes, and acknowledge what they learned from mistakes.

## Technical Leadership Without Coding

A persistent challenge for engineering managers is maintaining technical credibility without being the bottleneck. Interviewers ask "How do you stay technical?" and "When was the last time you overruled an architectural decision?" They are probing whether the candidate can participate meaningfully in design reviews, ask the right questions to expose risks, and trust their team's technical judgment while still setting guardrails.

Evaluation criteria: ability to assess technical risk without writing the code, knowing when to dive deep vs. delegate, setting engineering standards that scale with the team, and making build-vs-buy decisions with appropriate business context. Great candidates describe how they create an environment where engineers make good technical decisions independently.

## Delivery and Execution

Interviewers need confidence that a candidate can ship reliably. Questions like "Your team is two weeks behind on a critical deadline — what do you do?" test execution skill. Strong candidates describe how they break work into milestones, identify risks early, maintain transparency with stakeholders, and adjust scope rather than quality when timelines compress.

Key areas: managing cross-team dependencies, running effective ceremonies without descending into status theater, balancing tech debt against feature delivery, and creating accountability without micromanagement. The best candidates understand that on-time delivery is a function of accurate estimation and scope management — not pressure.

## Conflict Resolution and Organizational Navigation

Engineering managers operate at the intersection of engineering, product, and business leadership. Interviewers present scenarios: "Two senior engineers on your team disagree on the architecture and it's blocking progress" or "Product wants to ship a feature your team believes is technically unsound." They evaluate whether the candidate can facilitate productive disagreement, make decisions when consensus is impossible, and maintain trust with both their team and their peers.

Great candidates demonstrate that they address conflict directly, seek to understand each party's underlying interests rather than their stated positions, and create structured decision-making processes (RFCs, design docs with explicit criteria) that reduce personal friction. They know when to escalate, when to shield their team, and when to let engineers engage directly with stakeholders.

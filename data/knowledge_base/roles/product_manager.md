# Product Manager — Interview Guide

## Product Sense and Strategy

Product sense is the single most scrutinized competency in PM interviews. Interviewers present open-ended prompts like "How would you improve Google Maps?" or "Design a product for elderly users to manage medications." They are not looking for a feature list. They are evaluating whether the candidate can identify a specific user segment, articulate their pain points with genuine empathy, and propose solutions that map to real user needs rather than personal preferences.

A strong answer follows a structure: define the user, understand the context and current alternatives, identify gaps, then propose solutions ranked by impact and feasibility. Great candidates go further — they explain how they would validate assumptions before building, what metrics would indicate success, and what risks could derail the initiative.

Common pitfall: defaulting to "add a feature" without diagnosing the problem. Another is proposing solutions that are technically impressive but disconnected from what users actually need. PMs who cannot articulate the "why" behind their proposals raise serious concerns.

## Prioritization and Execution

Every PM interview includes some form of prioritization question. Interviewers want to see that candidates can navigate tradeoffs between competing stakeholder demands, technical constraints, and business goals. Frameworks like RICE or ICE are fine as scaffolding, but what matters is the reasoning underneath. A candidate who says "I'd use RICE scoring" without explaining how they estimate impact or confidence is performing a ritual, not demonstrating judgment.

Evaluation criteria: ability to define clear criteria, handle ambiguity, say no to good ideas in favor of better ones, and communicate the rationale for tradeoffs. Strong candidates also address sequencing — what to build first and why — and acknowledge dependencies between workstreams.

Red flag: treating prioritization as a purely analytical exercise. Real prioritization involves navigating organizational dynamics, aligning incentives, and building consensus across engineering, design, and leadership.

## Stakeholder Management and Communication

PMs operate at the intersection of engineering, design, business, and customers. Interview questions like "Tell me about a time you disagreed with an engineer" or "How do you handle a request from the CEO that conflicts with your roadmap?" probe whether a candidate can influence without authority, manage conflict constructively, and communicate clearly to audiences with different levels of technical depth.

Great candidates listen first, seek to understand the other party's constraints, and find solutions that address the underlying need rather than the surface request. They demonstrate self-awareness about their communication style.

## Metrics and Data-Driven Thinking

PMs must define what success looks like in measurable terms. Interviewers ask "How would you measure the success of this feature?" and evaluate whether the candidate can identify leading and lagging indicators, distinguish vanity metrics from actionable ones, and design experiments (A/B tests) with proper controls. Strong candidates discuss guardrail metrics — things that should not get worse — alongside primary success metrics. They also reason about metric tradeoffs: optimizing for engagement might hurt retention, and a good PM recognizes this tension.
